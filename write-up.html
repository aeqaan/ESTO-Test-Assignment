Reminder with a way out

Every reminder comes with a way out. A free email reminder 3 days before and on the due date: customers who forgot get a Pay button right in the email; customers short of money this month see every help option their agreement allows — payment holiday, lower monthly payment, asking for a new date or contacting ESTO's team — in one place, before they're late.

The problem and who has it

I couldn't find a free reminder before the due date, and help for a hard month sits in Invoices and Agreements, where the customer has to find it (UX audit hypothesis — from my review of an empty customer account and the portal's on-screen text).

  • The invoice comes by email on the 1st; payment is due on the 15th. The only reminder before the due date I could find is paid — SMS in ESTO Premium, €1.99 a month. After the due date, some reminders cost a fee.
  • Payment holidays and a lower monthly payment (for eligible hire purchase) already exist, but sit in Invoices and Agreements — the customer has to log in and find them (UX audit hypothesis). Otherwise the customer has to email the debt team.
  • For ESTO, an overdue invoice blocks credit line withdrawals (per the portal's on-screen text), so the customer takes no new money from the line and creates no new interest on it (inference); interest is about 80% of revenue (first nine months of 2026, my calculation). Long late payments can end in debt sale: ESTO sells overdue debts and books a loss (price and timing not disclosed). CCD2 (the EU's revised Consumer Credit Directive) requires lenders, where appropriate, to show reasonable forbearance before enforcement: Latvia and Lithuania have adopted national laws; Estonia's is planned from 20 May 2027, if adopted.

Who has it. A late payment has two different causes — the customer forgot, or money is short this month — and each needs a different fix (my model). Customers who pay on time are the third group: they get almost nothing new, and they're where I check the change does no harm. ESTO doesn't disclose how many customers are in each group, and a customer can't be labelled upfront; afterwards, ESTO data gives a proxy: a late payment fully closed within 1–5 days after the 15th ≈ forgot, a longer or partial one ≈ short of money.

Why free, and why a way out. Nobody plans to forget, so a paid reminder misses the people who need it most — my hypothesis, and the test checks it. A reminder without a way out can move the debt elsewhere — my inference from one field study: reminders cut card late fees by 14%, but people with past overdrafts paid 5% more in total fees (Medina, 2021).

Hypotheses

Two hypotheses — one for each group that needs a change (numbers are from my list of 40 hypotheses on the research board).

Needs and wants below are my assumptions — no customer interviews yet. I’d check them in 5–8 interviews with customers who were late before.

Hypothesis 13 · People short of money

AudienceCustomers who are short of money this month.
NeedsA way to stay on track without shame, and to know their options before they're late.
ProblemPayment holidays and a lower monthly payment sit in Invoices and Agreements, where the customer has to find them (UX audit hypothesis); a new date means emailing the debt team.
WantsA calm, clear option before things get worse.
Business problemLong late payments can end in debt sale: ESTO sells overdue debts and books a loss (price and timing not disclosed). CCD2 requires lenders, where appropriate, to show reasonable forbearance before enforcement (Latvia and Lithuania have adopted national laws; Estonia's is planned from 20 May 2027, if adopted).
Business goalFewer long late payments, and help requests before the due date rather than after.
HypothesisIf every reminder lists the help options the customer's agreement allows, and a button opens a short flow that suggests an option and shows its terms before confirming, then more customers who are short of money ask for help before the due date and fewer fall 30+ days behind, because today these options sit in the portal and are offered late (UX audit hypothesis), and a reminder without a way out can move the debt elsewhere (inference from Medina, 2021).
Why I expect it to workHelp arrives inside the reminder, at the moment of need. One question suggests an option; the other options the agreement allows stay visible, and “Contact our team” is always there; terms show before the customer confirms. Riskiest assumption: customers short of money realise it before the 15th and open ESTO's emails — checked first with ESTO data on when help requests arrive.
Success criteriaMore help requests before the 15th; fewer invoices 30+ days late. Later: fewer customers reaching 45+ days late (after 45 days ESTO may report the debt to the payment default register), and less debt sold. Guardrails: help taken by customers who always paid on time (a sign of misuse), the debt team's queue, repeat requests.
TestA/B 2×2 over 2 billing cycles: reminders yes / no × help block yes / no, customers randomised (see Impact). H13 compares the groups with the help block vs without. Before that: ESTO data on when help requests arrive today, and 5–8 interviews with customers who were late before — 3–4 who paid within 1–5 days, 3–4 who took longer.

Hypothesis 10 · People who forgot

AudienceCustomers without Premium who can pay but forget.
NeedsTo know what to pay and when, and to pay fast.
ProblemThe invoice arrives on the 1st; the only reminder before the 15th I could find is paid SMS in Premium.
WantsNo fees and, on the credit line, no blocked withdrawals — without effort.
Business problemAn overdue invoice blocks credit line withdrawals, so the customer takes no new money from the line and creates no new interest on it (inference) — and interest is about 80% of revenue.
Business goalMore invoices paid on time.
HypothesisIf every customer without Premium gets a free email reminder 3 days before and on the due date, with a Pay button, then more invoices are paid by the 15th, with the effect at least as strong among customers who were late before, because the only reminder before the due date I could find is a paid SMS in ESTO Premium, and nobody plans to forget.
Why I expect it to workReminders have the strongest evidence I found: in a field experiment with a microlender in Uganda, SMS reminders had the same average effect as cutting interest by a quarter, and the probability of paying on time rose 7–9% (relative) across the test groups (NBER, 2011). That evidence is for SMS, not email — so step 2 of the test (see Impact) checks the channel. A free reminder reaches the people who don't buy one upfront — my assumption, checked in the “late before” group.
Success criteriaShare of invoices paid by the 15th: +5 percentage points vs control (my target). The effect among customers who were late before is not below average. Fewer late payments closed 1–5 days after the 15th. Guardrails: Premium cancellations, unsubscribes, complaints.
TestThe same 2×2 A/B — groups with reminders vs without; then a channel test, email vs SMS (see Impact).

How I would measure the impact

Main metric: share of invoices paid on time, A/B tested against a control group over 2 billing cycles; target +5 percentage points (my estimate for the test design). “On time” means by the 15th, or by the new date if ESTO set one after a request from the flow — counted separately, so help doesn't look like a missed payment.

  • Customers who forgot: fewer late payments closed 1–5 days after the 15th.
  • Customers short of money: more help requests before the due date; fewer customers reaching 1–30 and 45+ days late.
  • Organised customers: no rise in Premium cancellations or unsubscribes.
  • Test, step 1 — reminder vs help (2×2, email only): control / help block in the invoice email only / reminders without help / reminders with help. This keeps the two effects apart: I expect reminders to cut short late payments and help to cut long ones.
  • Test, step 2 — channel, on the winning setup: email vs SMS. Email is free for the customer, and ESTO already sends the invoice by email. The strongest evidence is for SMS: reminders had the same average effect as cutting interest by a quarter, and the probability of paying on time rose 7–9% (relative) across the test groups (NBER, 2011). I found none for email, so I test it.
  • Who is in it: customers are randomised, not invoices. Premium subscribers are analysed separately — they already get SMS, and they are where I check for cancellations.
  • Sample size: set from the baseline. If 80% of invoices are paid on time today (an assumption), +5 points needs about 900 invoices per group (my calculation: α 0.05, power 80%). Customers, not invoices, are randomised, so the final size is adjusted for several invoices per customer (common practice). Rare outcomes like 45+ days late go to the 6–12 month business check.
  • Decision rule: ship if the main metric improves significantly and no guardrail crosses its threshold. If only reminders work, ship them and rework the help flow; if only help works, keep it in the invoice email.
  • Guardrails: Premium cancellations, complaints, unsubscribes, the debt team's queue, customers who got help and still reached 45+ days late, repeat help requests. Thresholds are set with risk and the debt team. Fee income isn't a guardrail — I expect it to fall, so it's in the business case.

How I would get it live

One country, a 2×2 test (three test groups and a control), two billing cycles. Nothing changes for the control group.

  • Involve: the Personal team lead and an engineer; the debt team — new-date requests land with them; compliance for a quick copy review (a shared platform team). Finance and data only for the baseline and the business case — with data access, I'd pull the baseline myself. Support gets a heads-up.
  • I'd build: the email template and copy, the help-flow screens with existing portal components, the experiment plan and analytics events, and usability tests of this prototype with 5–8 customers who were late before (3–4 who paid within 1–5 days, 3–4 who took longer).
  • Engineers build: reminder sending and A/B assignment, a link to the right invoice after Smart-ID / Mobile-ID login, eligibility rules per agreement, saving the choice and routing new-date requests to the debt team.
  • Rollout: week 1 baseline and sample size → weeks 2–3 build and test → two billing cycles of A/B step 1 in Estonia (about 70% of the portfolio at 30 September 2026, so the result applies to most of the portfolio) → channel test, then Latvia and Lithuania, which have adopted national CCD2 laws, with local copy and a compliance review → business check at 6 and 12 months.

How I used AI

  • Tools: Claude as a research and build partner — it read ESTO's reports, the reports of 20 competitors and the draft law text, and coded this prototype from my direction.
  • What worked: speed on primary sources, and one rule: every number is a sourced fact, a calculation with its formula, or a labelled assumption.
  • What I threw away: facts that failed a check — “87% of purchases are offline” (the survey counted people, not purchases), “up to 8% cashback” at Zilch (now up to 5–6%), a law date from the wrong bill, a study on money worries and attention (Mani et al., 2013) that failed to replicate.

Bonus: rough business case

Give up part of the fee income — its size isn't disclosed — for more interest income and lower losses (my hypothesis, checked at 6 and 12 months).

  • Why it can pay back: while an invoice is overdue, credit line withdrawals are blocked, so the customer takes no new money from the line and creates no new interest on it (inference). Interest is about 80% of revenue (€24.4M of €30.5M, first nine months of 2026). Annualised that's about €32.6M, so +1% interest income is about +€0.33M a year (my calculation). That counts all products; the effect should land mostly on the credit line, whose share isn't disclosed (inference).
  • The condition: ESTO doesn't disclose fee income. At Sezzle, whose pay-in-4 is interest-free, consumer fees were $131.9M in 2025, about 29% of revenue (my calculation) — so I can't assume ESTO's fees are small. First step: get the yearly fee income from finance.

Extra interest − funding cost + lower debt losses > lost fees + lost Premium revenue + sending costs

  • Measure in the same cohorts at 6 and 12 months: net revenue per customer = interest + fees + Premium − losses − funding and sending costs. ESTO's cost of funds is roughly 9.7% a year (my rough calculation from the 9M 2026 report).
  • Where it's strongest (inference): the credit line. On hire purchase and loans the gain comes only from repeat purchases. Lower losses should come mainly from the help flow — in my model, customers who forgot pay within days anyway.
  • Not counted: Premium offered later to customers back on schedule; cheaper funding from lower credit risk.
