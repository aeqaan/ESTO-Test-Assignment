Reminder with a way out

A free reminder before the due date, with help built into every reminder. Customers who forgot pay in one tap. Customers short of money this month see their options before they're late.

The problem and who has it

I couldn't find a free reminder before the due date, and help for a hard month sits deep in the portal.

  • The invoice comes by email on the 1st; payment is due on the 15th. The only reminder before the due date I could find is paid — SMS in ESTO Premium, €1.99 a month. After the due date, some reminders cost a fee.
  • Payment holidays and a lower monthly payment already exist, but sit deep in the portal. Otherwise the customer has to email the debt team.
  • For ESTO, an unpaid invoice pauses credit line withdrawals, and interest is about 80% of revenue. Long late payments end in debt sale, booked as a loss. CCD2 also asks lenders to offer forbearance before enforcement (Latvia and Lithuania from 20 November 2026; Estonia planned for 20 May 2027).
WhoWhat changes
Can pay, forgotA free reminder 3 days before and on the due date, with one-tap Pay
Can pay, organisedNothing — the group where we check we cause no harm
Short of money this monthEvery reminder shows the options and opens a short flow with clear terms

Why free, and why a way out. Nobody plans to forget, so a paid reminder misses the people who need it most — my hypothesis, and the test checks it. A reminder without a way out just moves the debt: in one field study, reminders cut card late fees by 14%, but people with past overdrafts paid 5% more in total fees (Medina, 2021).

How I would measure the impact

Main metric: share of invoices paid on time, A/B tested against a control group over 2 billing cycles; target +5 percentage points. “On time” means by the 15th, or by the new date if ESTO set one after a request from the flow — counted separately, so help doesn't look like a missed payment.

  • Forgot: fewer late payments closed 1–5 days after the 15th.
  • Short of money: more help requests before the due date; fewer customers reaching 1–30 and 45+ days late.
  • Organised: no rise in Premium cancellations or unsubscribes.
  • Test, step 1 — reminder vs help (2×2, email only): control / help block in the invoice email only / reminders without help / reminders with help. This keeps the two effects apart: I expect reminders to cut short late payments and help to cut long ones.
  • Test, step 2 — channel, on the winning setup: email / email + web push / SMS. Email is free and every customer has it. The strongest evidence is for SMS: reminders worked as well as cutting interest by a quarter, and on-time payment rose 7–9% across the test groups (NBER, 2011). There's none for email, so I test it.
  • Who is in it: customers are randomised, not invoices. Premium subscribers are analysed separately — they already get SMS, and they are where I check for cancellations.
  • Sample size: set from the baseline. If 80% of invoices are paid on time today (an assumption), +5 points needs about 900 invoices per group. Rare outcomes like 45+ days late go to the 6–12 month business check.
  • Decision rule: ship if the main metric improves significantly and no guardrail crosses its threshold. If only reminders work, ship them and rework the help flow; if only help works, keep it in the invoice email.
  • Guardrails: Premium cancellations, complaints, unsubscribes, the debt team's queue, customers who got help and still reached 45+ days late, repeat help requests. Thresholds are set with risk and the debt team. Fee income isn't a guardrail — I expect it to fall, so it's in the business case.

How I would get it live

One country, one test group, two billing cycles. Nothing changes for the control group.

  • Involve: the Personal team (owns the metric), the debt team (rules and new-date requests), risk, compliance (CCD2 and copy in three languages), finance and data (baseline and fee income), CRM and support.
  • I'd build: the email template and copy, the help-flow screens with existing portal components, usability tests of this prototype with 5–8 customers who were late before, the experiment plan and analytics events.
  • Engineers build: reminder sending and A/B assignment, a link to the right invoice after Smart-ID / Mobile-ID login, eligibility rules per agreement, saving the choice and routing new-date requests to the debt team.
  • Rollout: week 1 baseline and sample size → weeks 2–3 build and test → two billing cycles of A/B step 1 in Estonia (about 70% of the portfolio, so the result covers most customers) → channel test, then Latvia and Lithuania, where CCD2 already applies, with a compliance review → business check at 6 and 12 months.

How I used AI

  • Tools: Claude as a research and build partner — it read ESTO's reports, the reports of 20 competitors and the draft law text, and coded this prototype from my direction.
  • What worked: speed on primary sources, and one rule: every number is a sourced fact, a calculation with its formula, or a labelled assumption.
  • What I threw away: facts that failed a check — “87% of purchases are offline” (the survey counted people, not purchases), “up to 8% cashback” (now up to 5–6%), a law date from the wrong bill, a well-known study that didn't replicate — and the idea of SMS reminders for everyone.

Bonus: rough business case

Give up part of the fee income — its size isn't disclosed — for interest income and lower losses.

  • Why it can pay back: a customer who is late can't withdraw from the credit line, so they don't create new interest. Interest is about 80% of revenue (€24.4M of €30.5M, first nine months of 2026). Annualised that's about €32.6M, so +1% interest income is about +€0.33M a year (my calculation). That counts all products; the effect lands mostly on the credit line, whose share isn't disclosed.
  • The condition: ESTO doesn't disclose fee income. At Sezzle, whose pay-in-4 is interest-free, consumer fees were $131.9M in 2025, about 29% of revenue — so I can't assume ESTO's fees are small. First step: get the yearly fee income from finance.

Extra interest − funding cost + lower debt losses > lost fees + lost Premium revenue + sending costs

  • Measure in the same cohorts at 6 and 12 months: net revenue per customer = interest + fees + Premium − losses − funding and sending costs. ESTO's cost of funds is roughly 9.7% a year (my estimate).
  • Where it's strongest: the credit line. On hire purchase and loans the gain comes only from repeat purchases. Lower losses come mainly from the help flow — customers who forgot pay within days anyway.
  • Not counted: Premium offered later to customers back on schedule; cheaper funding from lower credit risk.
